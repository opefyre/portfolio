A third-party logistics provider, or 3PL, performs agreed logistics work for another business, such as warehousing, fulfilment or transport. A fourth-party logistics provider, or 4PL, coordinates a wider arrangement across logistics providers and activities.

[DHL describes a 4PL](https://lot.dhl.com/glossary/4pl/) as a coordinating contact that may manage 3PLs. It also says a 4PL may or may not own logistics assets. Asset ownership alone is a weak way to classify the arrangement.

The distinction becomes clearer when an order fails between the warehouse and the carrier. Someone needs to own that exception across both services.

## Follow the same order through two arrangements

The [ERP, WMS and TMS Note](/notes/erp-vs-wms-vs-tms/) follows a fictional 40-unit order. ERP records the business order, the warehouse management system directs picking, and the transport management system records carrier and delivery events.

Those are system responsibilities. A 3PL or 4PL describes an outsourced service role. Buying a TMS does not make a business a 4PL.

For the comparison below, keep the 40-unit order and change only who coordinates its warehouse and transport work. These are alternative service arrangements, not extra events claimed in the original case.

| Work or decision | Company coordinates its logistics providers | Company appoints a coordinating 4PL |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Pick and pack the 40 units | Contracted warehouse provider performs the work | Contracted warehouse provider still performs the work |
| Move the consignment | Contracted transport provider performs the movement | Contracted transport provider still performs the movement |
| Connect warehouse readiness with carrier booking | Company's logistics team coordinates the handoff | 4PL coordinates it within the agreed remit |
| Resolve a missed handoff across providers | Company leads the cross-provider investigation | 4PL leads it where the contract assigns that responsibility |
| Decide service policy and spending authority | Company retains its agreed business decisions | Company retains them, with any delegation stated explicitly |

A provider can combine several services. The table describes responsibilities to check, rather than a required commercial structure.

## A coordination contract still needs an operating boundary

Suppose the warehouse records the order as packed, but the transport record has no departure. Start by checking the physical handoff and its timestamp. A packed status does not prove the carrier collected the shipment.

The service agreement should identify who investigates, who can approve another collection, who tells the customer and which event closes the issue. A single contact without those decisions may simply relay messages between providers.

The [supplier-scorecard example](/notes/supplier-scorecard-example/) helps define delivery evidence. Measure the whole commitment where the coordinator owns it, while keeping each provider's event records available for investigation.

## Where 2PL fits

[DHL uses 2PL](https://dhl-freight-connections.com/en/business/1pl-2pl-3pl-4pl-5pl-logistics-models-compared/) for a provider supplying a specific transport service, such as carriage on its own network. The boundaries between provider labels vary, especially when one company sells several services.

For a selection decision, describe the actual purchase: carriage, warehouse fulfilment, coordination, planning, or a combination. Avoid deciding whether a large logistics company is “a 3PL or a 4PL” without naming the service being contracted.

There is no need to add a 5PL tier to explain the responsibility gap in this order.

## Choose the arrangement around the work you can own

A business with the people and information to coordinate providers may retain that role. Another may need a partner to manage handoffs, reporting and operating exceptions across a larger network.

Compare the coordinator's authority, access to events, ability to change providers, charging basis and exit arrangements. Check what remains with your team, including policy decisions and supplier relationships.

The [network-design Note](/notes/supply-chain-network-design/) covers where facilities and routes should sit. Outsourcing coordination does not settle those design choices or make a poor route feasible.

Take one recent order exception into the supplier discussion. Ask each party to walk through who detects it, decides, acts and confirms recovery. Record the answer beside the service scope before comparing the contract prices.
