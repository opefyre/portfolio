“Fix the late orders” sounds like a clear assignment until the team tries to book warehouse time, change a system or spend money on equipment. People support the aim. They disagree about what has been approved.

A **project charter formally authorises a project and gives its project manager authority to use organisational resources within agreed limits**. It establishes the purpose, high-level scope and responsibilities before the detailed delivery plan is ready.

[PMI's explanation of project charters](https://www.pmi.org/learning/library/charter-selling-project-7473) puts that authority at the centre. The project manager can draft the document; someone with the appropriate authority needs to approve and support it.

A long description of the problem won't settle who may take an operator off the line for a trial. The charter should make that kind of decision easier.

## Business case, charter and project plan

These documents can share information. They serve different decisions.

| Document | Main question | For our warehouse project |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Business case | Is the proposed investment worth pursuing? | Compare options, expected service improvement, costs and risks |
| Project charter | What project is authorised, and who can act? | Approve a bounded pilot, assign a sponsor and manager, set resource limits |
| Project plan | How will we deliver and control the agreed work? | Define deliverables, dependencies, dates, responsibilities and checks |

A charter may summarise the rationale, budget and milestones. A plan may refine those estimates as the team learns more. Neither document makes an unapproved purchase acceptable simply by listing it.

Our fictional distributor wants fewer orders to miss their promised dispatch date. The sponsor is willing to fund a small investigation and pilot. That doesn't yet authorise a warehouse-wide rollout or a new management system.

## A short project charter, filled in

The baseline is 80 late dispatches among 1,000 orders due in a four-week period, or 8%. An order is late if it leaves after its confirmed dispatch deadline. It is counted once, even if several lines are delayed.

The draft objective is at most 4% late dispatches in a four-week pilot review, without increasing the picking-error rate above the measured 1% baseline. These are fictional management targets, not a forecast of what the project will achieve.

:::card Warehouse dispatch pilot
**Purpose.** Investigate and reduce late dispatches for standard parcel orders in warehouse zone A.

**Scope.** Confirm the delay pattern, test one agreed routing or work-method change, train the affected team and report the pilot result. Zone B, supplier lead times and warehouse-system replacement are excluded.

**Sponsor and manager.** The operations director sponsors the project. The improvement lead manages it. Warehouse and IT managers confirm the staff time they can supply.

**Limits.** Up to €8,000 total pilot spend and eight calendar weeks. The manager may commit approved in-scope pilot purchases within that cap, following normal purchasing rules. The cap is not permission to change safety or financial controls.

**Review.** At week eight, the sponsor decides whether to stop, extend or authorise wider deployment. A process owner must accept any method retained after the pilot.
:::

This is an illustrative charter, not a completed approval record. A real version needs named people, dates and recorded approval under the organisation's rules.

Notice what it leaves open. The team hasn't committed to buying software or changing the warehouse layout. It has authority to investigate the delay and test a supported option within a boundary.

The [data quality checks](/notes/data-quality-dimensions/) are relevant before accepting that 8% baseline. A missing deadline or a revised promise date could change which orders count as late.

## What can the manager approve?

Try a few requests against this charter. The distinction becomes clearer when the request is specific.

::interactive charter-authority | Fictional authority checks against the charter above. €5,500 has already been spent or committed in every scenario. Requests are separate alternatives, not cumulative purchases. The manager can approve only in-scope pilot purchases within the €8,000 total cap, subject to normal controls. Sponsor decisions may also need Finance, IT or other approval.

A €1,200 label trial in zone A brings the total to €6,700. If it meets the purchasing and technical checks, it fits the example's delegated scope and budget.

The same amount spent on zone B is a different decision. It is outside the authorised area even though the total stays below €8,000. Remaining budget doesn't expand the project boundary.

A €3,000 zone A request would bring total commitments to €8,500. It needs a revised funding decision, a smaller proposal or an agreed reduction elsewhere. Calling the purchase essential doesn't change the arithmetic.

This is why the charter needs both scope and authority. An amount alone leaves the team guessing about which work it can commission.

## Keep the detailed work in the plan

Once the pilot is authorised, the team can break down its deliverables: a checked baseline, a tested method, trained users and an accepted handover. The [work breakdown structure example](/notes/work-breakdown-structure-example/) explains that step.

The plan then sets the activities, dependencies and dates needed to produce those deliverables. [Critical path](/notes/critical-path-method-worked-example/) helps assess which dependencies govern the finish date. A [RACI matrix](/notes/raci-matrix-guide/) clarifies responsibilities for the work and decisions.

Those details should agree with the charter. Assigning IT an implementation task doesn't create IT capacity. In this example, its manager still needs to confirm the allocation. A RACI label cannot supply authority that nobody has granted.

Keep enough detail in the charter to expose a major constraint. Leave the daily task sequence in the plan, where it can be maintained without rewriting the project's purpose after every scheduling change.

## A target needs a review method

At the review, show the late count and all orders due in the period. A pilot with 24 late dispatches out of 600 is at 4%. So is one with 40 out of 1,000. The rate helps compare different volumes; the counts show the size of the evidence.

Record picking errors over the same defined population and check the order mix. A lower late rate alongside easier orders or more overtime needs explanation before wider deployment. The charter's target is a decision aid, not proof that the pilot caused a result.

For a small work-method trial, [PDCA](/notes/pdca-cycle-example/) supplies a way to plan, test and review the proposed change. Use the [benefits realisation approach](/notes/benefits-realisation-automation/) to keep ownership and later performance visible after project delivery.

The sponsor can authorise an extension when the evidence is promising but incomplete. The week-eight review does not have to become an automatic rollout.

## When the assignment changes

Suppose the investigation finds that the dispatch problem comes from promises made before warehouse work begins. The original pilot may no longer reach the cause.

Bring that finding to the sponsor. Explain the new boundary, resource needs and effect on the objective before committing the team to wider work. Update the approval and the plan together if the assignment changes.

Before committing to the wider work, use the charter to confirm what the manager may approve and which decision belongs with the sponsor. Record any revised authority with the approved scope.
