An order waits two days before packing. Once packed, some boxes contain the wrong quantity. The team could start a Lean project, a Six Sigma project or a Kaizen event.

It would help to separate the waiting from the quantity errors first.

**Kaizen means ongoing improvement involving the people doing the work. Lean looks at delivering customer value with less waste and better flow. Six Sigma focuses on reducing variation and defects through disciplined investigation.** A team can use them together: Kaizen brings people into the improvement, while flow and defect analysis help explain different parts of the problem.

[Lean Enterprise Institute](https://www.lean.org/lexicon-terms/kaizen/) describes Kaizen as more than a one-off event. [ASQ](https://asq.org/quality-resources/six-sigma) distinguishes Lean's focus on flow and waste from Six Sigma's focus on variation and process control.

## Follow the order before choosing a method

In our fictional packing area, completed picks wait in a large batch for the next station. Packers then count parts by hand. Both issues affect the customer, but they need different evidence.

::figure paa-lean-order | Fictional process sketch. Waiting before packing and errors within packing are different problems. No cycle-time reduction or defect result is assumed from either proposed change.

The team can observe when picks become ready and when packing starts. If waiting grows because work is transferred only once per shift, it can test a smaller transfer quantity, after checking capacity and handling requirements.

For wrong quantities, it needs the error definition, counts and circumstances: product, pack size, equipment and method. Buying a scale would be premature if the errors come from the wrong order information.

| Approach | What it helps ask here | Work it could lead to |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Kaizen | What can the people doing the work test and improve? | Operators and the supervisor trial a clearer handoff and review the result |
| Lean | Where does the order wait or receive work the customer does not need? | Examine batch transfers, queues and the whole route |
| Six Sigma | Why do packed quantities vary from the requirement? | Check measurement, investigate sources of error and control the verified change |

In either investigation, collect enough evidence to choose and check the change. A clear local correction may need much less analysis than errors that vary across products and shifts.

## Five Lean principles describe a way to think

The [five principles associated with Womack and Jones](https://www.lean.org/lexicon-terms/lean-thinking-and-practice/) concern customer value, the value stream, flow, pull and continued pursuit of a better process.

For our order, that means agreeing what the customer needs, following the full route, reducing interruptions where feasible and linking work to downstream need. The next review asks what remains unresolved.

Pull does not mean every business must wait for a final customer order before buying anything. A replenishment signal can connect stages. The [Kanban example](/notes/kanban-pull-system/) explains one such operating rule.

Seven wastes are a different list: categories used to notice waste, not seven replacement Lean principles. The [eight-wastes note](/notes/eight-wastes-lean/) explains the commonly added eighth category. An organisation using a different principle list should name the model it follows.

## Where 5S and a Kaizen event fit

[5S](/notes/5s-methodology-example/) helps organise and maintain the workplace so needed items and abnormal conditions are easier to identify. It supports Lean work, but a tidy packing bench alone will not remove a queue created by the transfer rule.

A Kaizen event gives a team concentrated time to investigate and test a change. Daily follow-up still matters. Someone has to maintain labels, train new people and check whether the change works during the busy shift.

Kaizen Institute publishes its own five-principle formulation around the customer, flow, direct observation, participation and transparency. [Its explanation](https://kaizen.com/what-is-kaizen/) is a named approach, not a universal five-step project sequence.

Seven-step Kaizen guides also vary by author; LEI's resource describes an eight-step problem-solving account. Use the version your team follows, with a clear current condition, test, result and follow-up.

## When to use a fuller investigation

If the packing errors recur across products and shifts and their cause remains unclear, [DMAIC](/notes/dmaic-step-by-step/) provides a structured route through Define, Measure, Analyze, Improve and Control. It can include a small [PDCA trial](/notes/pdca-cycle-example/) of the proposed fix.

If the cause is already verified and the change is straightforward, the team may need an authorised correction and a result check. It should not create a long project merely to use a preferred method.

For an accessible first look, Vrolen's [PDCA production example](https://vrolen.com/blog/pdca-cycle/) shows how a trial connects a proposed change to its review.

Start the next discussion with the order, delay or defect you can observe. Choose the amount of investigation the uncertainty requires, then keep responsibility for the result with the operating team. The method is useful when it helps them make a better decision.
