The packing bench is clean at the start of the shift. The scanner has been borrowed, two versions of the shipping label sit in the same tray, and nobody knows who refills the spare roll.

The team has tidied the area. The next person still has to work out how to use it.

**5S is a method for organising and maintaining a workplace so the required items and conditions are easy to see.** Its five steps are Sort, Set in order, Shine, Standardize and Sustain. [ASQ explains the five steps](https://asq.org/quality-resources/five-s-tutorial), including cleaning as a chance to inspect the workplace.

The [Lean Enterprise Institute](https://www.lean.org/lexicon-terms/five-s/) places 5S within visual control and stable work. A one-off cleanup leaves much of that job unfinished.

This fictional packing station shows how the five steps fit together.

## Start with the work the station must support

The operator needs to identify the order, scan the carton, apply the correct label and confirm it before dispatch. The station needs a scanner, a printer, the current label stock and packing supplies.

The starting problems are specific. The scanner has no agreed return location. An obsolete label roll looks similar to the current one. Replacement stock appears whenever someone remembers to fetch it.

Putting a bright outline around every object won't settle which label belongs here or when a refill is due. Those decisions come first.

| Step | Meaning | Decision at this station |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Sort | Separate what is needed from what isn't | Identify the old label roll and remove it from active use for an authorised disposition |
| Set in order | Arrange needed items for use and return | Give the scanner a named home; separate the working roll and reserve |
| Shine | Clean and inspect | Check the printer and scanner condition, then deal with the source of recurring dirt or damage |
| Standardize | Make the agreed condition repeatable | Show the current label specification, locations, refill trigger and responsible roles |
| Sustain | Keep using and improving the arrangement | Check readiness at shift handover and follow up on repeated problems |

Sort is not permission to discard every item used infrequently. Emergency equipment, retained records and tools for occasional work may need a defined place elsewhere. Check their purpose and retention requirements with the responsible person.

## Give each location a meaning

The proposed station below separates active supplies from a reserve, identifies the approved label and makes the scanner's absence visible. It shows relationships, not a measured floor plan or an approved ergonomic design.

Both layouts stay visible. Change the proposed station's condition to see what the team would notice and what it should do next.

An empty scanner home should prompt a known response. In this example, the operator checks the recorded loan and contacts the shift lead if it is unresolved. A spare can be used only under the site's approved method. A missing scanner doesn't justify bypassing the verification step.

An empty reserve position raises a different issue. The working roll may still be in use, but replacement stock needs attention. Here, moving the reserve roll into use creates a refill request assigned to the materials runner. The team needs to confirm that the refill can arrive before the next shortage.

If that timing is unreliable, painting the shelf won't solve it. The [Kanban example](/notes/kanban-pull-system/) explains how consumption, replenishment time and container quantity affect a repeatable refill loop.

## Standardize the response as well as the appearance

The station standard needs enough detail for the next shift to act. A photo can show where the scanner belongs. It cannot, on its own, tell someone whether the label roll is the current revision.

For this station, keep four details beside the layout: the approved label identity, the tool return location, the refill trigger and the person to contact when the condition is wrong. Maintain the revision and show where exceptions are recorded.

The [standard work vs SOP comparison](/notes/standard-work-vs-sop/) covers the wider work sequence and checks. 5S supports that method by making its tools and materials available. A tidy station still needs a clear way to scan, label and verify the carton.

Avoid adding a sign for every imaginable exception. Watch what people repeatedly have to ask, then make that answer easier to find.

## Check whether the new arrangement helps

Before changing the station, observe several representative shifts. Record tool searches, trips for labels, wrong-label incidents and interruptions caused by missing supplies. Keep the order count and mix alongside them so a quieter shift doesn't look like an improvement by itself.

After the trial, use the same definitions and observation method. Check with operators whether the new homes are reachable, whether refills arrive and whether other work has become awkward. A printer moved closer may obstruct a carton route; fix the arrangement before standardising it.

Minutes spent searching could become time available for other work. That doesn't automatically reduce payroll or increase shipments. Follow the effect into the work cycle before calling it a financial benefit.

A small [PDCA trial](/notes/pdca-cycle-example/) is enough to structure that comparison. There is no need to invent a percentage improvement for the launch slide.

## Sustain: make the shift check worth doing

At handover, check whether the scanner is available, the label identity is current, supplies can last until the next refill and the equipment is fit to use. Resolve an exception or assign it to someone who can.

If the scanner is missing every morning, investigate the borrowing and return arrangement. Repeatedly restoring the same empty outline is evidence that the method needs attention. It isn't a reason to add another weekly photograph.

[Vrolen's 5S guide](https://vrolen.com/blog/5s-methodology/) expands on choosing work measures and maintaining the arrangement.
