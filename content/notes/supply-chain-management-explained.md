A customer orders 100 replacement pumps for delivery on Friday. The sales system accepts the date. The warehouse has 60 usable pumps. The next supplier delivery is due on Monday.

The customer has one order. Inside the business, several teams now have decisions to make.

**Supply chain management coordinates how a business meets demand: planning, sourcing, making or buying, moving goods and dealing with returns.** It also connects the information and payments those activities depend on. [CSCMP's definition](https://cscmp.org/CSCMP/Educate/SCM_Definitions_and_Glossary_of_Terms.aspx) includes coordination within the company and with suppliers, service providers and customers.

For this fictional order, the Friday promise depends on decisions across all those activities.

## Start with the promise

Before confirming Friday, the planner needs to know what is available, what is already committed and when more stock can actually be used. A delivery arriving at the gate may still need inspection before it can fulfil an order.

Sales also needs to know whether the customer accepts a split delivery. Sixty pumps on Friday and 40 on Monday may solve the customer's problem, or leave an entire maintenance job waiting.

A forecast helps prepare stock before orders arrive. It is not another order to add on top of the same demand. The [sales and operations planning example](/notes/sales-operations-planning-explained/) connects expected demand with supply and capacity over a longer horizon.

## Purchasing and logistics handle different parts

Procurement arranges the supply: which pump meets the requirement, who can provide it, on what terms and with what reliability. Logistics manages movement and storage, including the return route. Supply chain management connects those choices with the customer's need and the rest of the business.

| Part of this order | Decision it needs |
| --- | --- |
| Plan | Can 100 usable pumps reach the customer by Friday? |
| Source | Can the supplier bring 40 forward, with the same approved specification? |
| Make or prepare | Are assembly, testing or packaging needed before dispatch? |
| Deliver | Which route meets the accepted date and quantity? |
| Return | Who checks, replaces or credits a damaged pump? |

These five headings are a way to explain this order. They are not a complete organisational chart. A distributor may buy finished pumps; a manufacturer may need materials, production capacity and a bill of materials. [Material requirements planning](/notes/material-requirements-planning-example/) covers that dependent-demand calculation.

## A cheaper response can cost more elsewhere

Suppose the supplier offers Friday delivery for €200 more than Monday. The buyer chooses Monday to save €200, then pays €350 for an express arrangement that gets the pumps there on Friday. Compared with accepting the Friday offer, the company spends €150 more.

The buyer may have met a purchase-price target. The customer promise and freight bill still belong in the decision. The [procurement savings example](/notes/when-procurement-savings-increase-total-cost/) follows that arithmetic in more detail.

Another possibility is to agree Monday with the customer before committing extra spend. That needs an honest discussion of the customer's consequences, not a quiet change to the promise date after the order is late.

The supply chain manager brings the alternatives together: service, inventory, capacity, cost and risk. They do not necessarily approve every purchase or drive every delivery. They need to make conflicts visible and bring decisions to someone with authority.

## What skills does that work need?

The planner needs to check whether the stock is usable and already allocated. The buyer may need to negotiate an earlier receipt. Sales needs to explain the available options and their consequences to the customer.

Those skills work together. Analysis identifies the 40-unit gap; product and process knowledge establish acceptable alternatives; coordination turns the chosen response into matching purchasing, warehouse and customer instructions.

At the review, check the accepted promise, the actual quantity and the extra cost. [On-time delivery and OTIF](/notes/otif-vs-on-time-delivery/) explain why shipping something on Friday is not the same as delivering the complete requirement.

## Why function lists have different counts

You may see supply chain management explained as five functions, five pillars or seven Cs. These lists group the work differently. Check which model a list belongs to before using it to assign responsibilities.

ASCM's current [SCOR Digital Standard](https://scor.ascm.org/) uses Orchestrate alongside Plan, Order, Source, Transform, Fulfill and Return. That is a named model, broader than the older five-process summary often reproduced online. Use the version your organisation has adopted when mapping its work.

One influential seven-principle account is Anderson, Britt and Favre's [1997 article](https://supplychainventure.com/PDF/TheSevenPrinciplesofSupplyChainManagement.pdf). In plain terms, it calls for grouping customers by service needs, adapting the logistics network, using demand signals in planning, delaying product differentiation where appropriate, sourcing strategically, connecting information across the chain and measuring performance across partners.

Those are management principles from a named article, not seven steps an order passes through. In our example, the service requirement, supply options and shared cost review put several of them into practice.

For a first review, take one order and follow its promise, stock, supply, delivery, invoice and any return. Ask where the records disagree and who can settle the difference. That is a manageable way to see the supply chain as a connected operation.
