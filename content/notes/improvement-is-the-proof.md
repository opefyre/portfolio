Most continuous-improvement work I've seen — on production lines at Unilever, in launches at BAT, in operations teams since — is organised around the *change*. Someone maps the process, someone finds the loss, a countermeasure gets agreed, a task gets closed. The dashboard turns green for a week.

What almost never gets the same attention is the question after that: **did it hold?**

## The cycle is split across too many tools

A typical improvement loop lives in five places at once. The process map is in one tool, the data in a spreadsheet, the analysis in a slide deck, the actions in a task tracker, and the result — if anyone checks — in a dashboard that doesn't know any of the rest happened.

Each of those tools is fine on its own. The problem is the seams between them. Evidence doesn't travel with the finding. The finding doesn't travel with the decision. And by the time anyone asks whether the countermeasure worked, the context that would let you answer is gone.

## Findings, not KPIs

A KPI tells you *that* something moved. It doesn't tell you *why*, or whether the thing you changed is the thing that moved it. An explainable finding does: this station, this loss, this evidence, this reason.

That is a different unit of work. It's also the one that can be verified later — because you wrote down what you expected to happen.

## Verification is the product

That's the idea behind [Vrolen](/work/vrolen/): connect the whole cycle — observation, evidence, finding, investigation, decision, countermeasure — and then keep going. Verify whether the change produced the expected result. If it did, keep it as a standard, so the organisation actually learns it. If it didn't, you find out while it's still cheap to find out.

Simulation has a place too, but a narrow one: when a proposed change is worth testing before you touch the real operation.

The change is the easy part. The proof is the improvement.
