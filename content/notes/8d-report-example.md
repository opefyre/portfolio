**An 8D report records an eight-discipline problem-solving response: define the problem, contain its effects, verify causes, implement a lasting correction and check that it works.** Some organisations add D0 for preparation before D1 to D8.

A customer finds twelve mounting plates with holes in the wrong position. The supplier offers to inspect the next delivery. That may protect the customer for now, but the process can still make the same fault.

[ASQ's 8D explanation](https://asq.org/quality-resources/eight-disciplines-8d) separates interim containment from permanent correction and later effectiveness checks. The report should let someone follow that evidence, rather than simply see that eight boxes were filled in.

## The 8D steps, with a filled-in report example

This fictional supplier shipped a lot of 1,000 plates. The customer identified twelve nonconforming units and returned the entire lot. Another 600 plates remain at the supplier. The investigation covers both groups.

The team inspects all 1,600 under a defined hole-position check. It finds twenty nonconforming plates in the returned lot, including the original twelve, and eight in plant stock. That is **28 nonconforming and 1,572 conforming units**, not forty failures.

| Step | Entry in this example's report |
| --- | --- |
| D0: prepare | Protect the affected customer and define the initial trace scope |
| D1: form the team | Quality lead, process engineer, production lead and customer contact own the response |
| D2: describe the problem | Wrong hole position on the specified plate; record drawing revision, lots and unit counts |
| D3: contain | Isolate the 1,600 units, inspect them and keep the 28 failed units segregated pending disposition |
| D4: verify causes | Confirm the locating fault that produced the error and the inspection omission that let it escape |
| D5: select and verify correction | Trial the revised fixture arrangement and the required hole-position check |
| D6: implement and validate | Release the approved changes and inspect three subsequent 1,000-unit lots under the agreed trial conditions |
| D7: prevent recurrence | Update the control plan, care tasks, inspection instruction and review of similar fixtures |
| D8: close and recognise the team | Review the evidence with the agreed owners, record closure and acknowledge the team's work |

Keep the report entries short and link to the supporting records: inspection results, trace lists, trial evidence, approvals and revised instructions. Each action also needs an owner, due date and current status.

Set response deadlines through the customer agreement and the applicable quality process.

## Containment vs corrective action: different jobs

Containment protects against the current problem while the cause is investigated. Here, isolation and added inspection control the affected stock. They do not repair the process that produced the wrong hole position.

Permanent corrective action addresses the verified cause. Replacing a worn locating component may form part of it, but a replacement record alone does not show that the fixture now holds position or will keep doing so.

The [cost-of-quality example](/notes/cost-of-quality-example/) separates inspection and failure costs from prevention. That distinction helps explain why paying for repeated sorting is different from stopping the recurrence.

The 28 nonconforming plates remain visible in the record until their approved disposition is complete. Finding the cause does not turn those units into good stock.

## Correction, corrective action and preventive action

**Correction addresses the detected nonconformity. Corrective action addresses its cause to prevent recurrence. Preventive action addresses a potential nonconformity before it occurs.** CAPA is shorthand for corrective and preventive action; its required procedure depends on the organisation and any applicable sector rules.

| Action in this plate example | What it addresses |
| --- | --- |
| Approved rework or other disposition of the 28 failed plates | The existing nonconforming output; sorting alone does not correct it |
| Restore the locating condition and maintain a verified control | The confirmed cause on the affected process, to prevent recurrence |
| Assess a similar fixture before it makes this fault and apply justified controls | A potential failure in another process, before occurrence there |

The [ASQ glossary](https://asq.org/quality-resources/quality-glossary) describes preventive action in terms of preventing occurrence. The 8D response ties the detected problem to containment, causes and effectiveness checks. This comparison explains the terms; it is not a regulated CAPA procedure or a universal five-step or seven-step sequence.

Use [FMEA](/notes/fmea-step-by-step/) when examining a potential failure in that similar fixture. Copying a correction without checking whether the cause exists there can add work without preventing the actual risk.

## Verify the occurrence cause and the escape cause

The **occurrence cause** explains why the fault was made. The **escape cause** explains why the existing controls did not stop it reaching the customer.

In this fictional investigation, the team verifies that excess movement in a locating component permits the plate to sit in the wrong position. Trial records compare the identified condition with the restored arrangement against the hole-position requirement.

The escape investigation finds a separate omission: the inspection instruction checks overall width but omits the hole's position from its reference edge. Retained failed parts pass the old width check and fail the required position check.

Those findings support two related actions. Restore and control the locating condition, and correct the inspection method. Retraining the inspector on the old instruction would leave the missing characteristic out of the check.

Use the [5 Whys example](/notes/5-whys-example/) to organise a cause chain, with evidence at each link. The explanation still needs verification; a plausible diagram is not the finding.

## D5 and D6: verify the proposal, then check it in use

D5 examines the proposed correction before relying on it. D6 implements the approved correction and checks its effectiveness in the operating conditions it is meant to cover.

For this case, the team agrees to check three further lots of 1,000, with the specified product, method and inspection coverage. The initial example records zero nonconforming units in each. The owners review the evidence before authorising containment removal.

The control changes one later lot's result. Zero findings under these trial conditions do not guarantee zero future failures, and containment release remains an authorised decision.

Change the second verification lot to one failure. The stated check is no longer met. Containment remains active and the report stays open while the team reviews the failure and the correction.

Do not relabel that failed check as passed because the fixture was installed on time. Installation is evidence of action. The subsequent output is evidence about the result.

## Close the report without losing the control

The [standard work and SOP note](/notes/standard-work-vs-sop/) explains where the updated method belongs. Record the applicable versions and make sure the people doing the work can use them.

D7 also asks whether the same cause can exist elsewhere. A review of similar fixtures may identify further work; one successful trial does not cover every other product.

If the team needs a short account of the wider problem and reasoning, the [A3 example](/notes/a3-problem-solving-example/) offers that format. The 8D record here keeps the complaint, containment and correction evidence connected.

For these plates, closure means the agreed evidence has been reviewed and the ongoing controls have owners. The aim is to make the next recurrence less likely and visible if it happens, rather than make the complaint disappear from the list.
